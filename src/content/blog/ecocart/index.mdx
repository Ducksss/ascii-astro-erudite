EcoCart was a browser extension and dashboard we built for LifeHack 2023. The idea was to bring environmental information onto a shopping page, while someone could still change their mind, and collect the resulting records in a separate dashboard.

Kei Lok Tham handled design and frontend work, Yu Hoe Tan handled AI and backend work, and I worked on the frontend and browser extension. Our team won the [grand prize and SGID award](https://github.com/keiloktql/LifeHack2023-EcoCart). The extension reached into a shop we did not control, and the apparent precision of its numbers exceeded the evidence behind them.

## Reading a shop from the outside

We could change our dashboard whenever we wanted. We could not change Shopee, so the extension had to read the page that the shopper saw.

On a product page, it collected the title, category breadcrumbs and details such as the shipping origin. It sent those to a backend endpoint and inserted a banner near the product title. The [content script](https://github.com/keiloktql/LifeHack2023-EcoCart/blob/bb1068be8965a74df0822303d4d17e627da6376a/extensions/src/foreground.ts) waited for the relevant page elements to appear before doing that work.

```text wrap=false
Shopee page -> product details -> backend response -> inline banner
```

The placement was the useful part. A separate website would have asked someone to copy a product name, leave the shop and research it. An inline banner could make the information available alongside the price and description.

It was also a fragile integration. The script depended on Shopee's CSS selectors and page structure, including generated class names. A shop redesign could break extraction without changing a line of our code. Supporting another marketplace would mean understanding another page structure, rather than simply adding its name to a list.

## From the cart to a history

The [cart script](https://github.com/keiloktql/LifeHack2023-EcoCart/blob/bb1068be8965a74df0822303d4d17e627da6376a/extensions/src/cart.ts) collected product titles, requested a combined emissions estimate and displayed it near checkout. Its custom checkout handler wrote a record to Supabase with the user ID, estimate, merchant and cart URL.

The Next.js dashboard read those records into a table and grouped their values by month for a chart. That gave the prototype a second view: the extension showed an individual shopping moment, while the dashboard showed an accumulated history.

There was an important limitation in the handoff. Clicking our button created the record, but it was not proof that the shop had completed a purchase. A reliable purchase history would need an order-confirmation signal and a way to handle cancellations, returns and repeated clicks. Otherwise, the chart can faithfully add up records that do not describe actual purchases.

## The estimate was the weakest link

The backend used GPT-3.5 Turbo to generate emissions information from product names and categories. The [cart-estimate prompt](https://github.com/keiloktql/LifeHack2023-EcoCart/blob/bb1068be8965a74df0822303d4d17e627da6376a/backend/functions/calculate-total-co/app.py) explicitly asked for a rough number even when the available information was limited.

A product title is not a record of its materials, manufacturing process or delivery route. Asking a model to fill those gaps made it easy to demonstrate the interface, but did not make the result a measured carbon footprint.

The code also used random-number fallbacks when generation or parsing failed. I would remove those before taking this beyond a demonstration. An unavailable estimate should remain unavailable, because a plausible number in a green banner invites people to treat it as evidence.

The same issue appeared in the preferred-brand badges. They came from a hard-coded list of brand names, rather than an assessment of the specific product. A badge might look like verification even though the script had only matched a word in the title.

## What I would keep

I would keep the extension's position in the shopping flow and the dashboard's simple history. I would change what they were allowed to claim.

A stronger version would show the source and assumptions behind an estimate, distinguish category-level information from product-specific data, and leave gaps visible. SGID could help with account verification, but it could not verify the environmental calculation. A chart could help someone explore their records, but it could not make uncertain inputs accurate by adding them together.
