I implemented two kinds of guidance in MetaLearner: a short onboarding modal over the BI dashboard and a User Guide for the details that would not fit comfortably in a tour.

They answer different questions. The tour helps someone recognise the interface in front of them, while the guide helps them do something with it. Explaining where the chat panel lives is a smaller task than explaining how to ask it for a useful comparison.

## A tour anchored to the dashboard

The modal used swipe gestures and step navigation to move through the introduction, with feature explanations and “Find out more” links into deeper guidance. It was prepared to work with the product's language support.

_The dashboard stays visible behind the introduction. The first step describes KPIs and immediate action items, giving the reader a reference for what they will see when the modal closes._

That placement has a useful tradeoff. Keeping the dashboard in view connects the explanation to the actual screen, but the modal also interrupts the screen. A tour should therefore carry enough information to orient someone, while leaving detailed instructions somewhere they can consult during a task.

The step controls help with pacing. Someone can move through the introduction at their own speed instead of reading a single wall of text, and the deeper links give a path beyond the summary.

## Four ways to enter the guide

The User Guide was organised around four categories:

| Category                    | Reader's task                                      |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Dashboard Overview          | Understand the layout, KPIs and basic interactions |
| Drill Down into the Details | Explore charts and inspect data more closely       |
| Ask and Extract Your Data   | Form a question and follow up on the response      |
| Settings                    | Find preferences and usage guidance                |

This gives the guide more than one entry point. Someone trying to interpret a chart does not need to read the assistant instructions first, and someone trying the assistant can go directly to question examples.

The content included suggested questions, examples of stronger and weaker prompts, and guidance for continuing a conversation across several turns. I reviewed the guidance against the product rather than treating it as a separate description of what the product might eventually do.

## Giving a question a scope

An illustrative prompt-writing example is the difference between “Show me costs” and “Compare shipping costs by supplier for March”. The second question names a measure, a grouping and a period, so there is less left for the assistant to infer.

That is a useful pattern to teach because it applies beyond one suggested question:

- Name the measure you want, such as shipping cost or expected demand.
- Name the grouping or filter, such as supplier or product category.
- Include a period when the question depends on time.

A follow-up can change one part of that scope. “Break that down by product” is easier to interpret after a supplier comparison than as an opening message with no context. It also gives the user a way to explore the answer without composing a large request containing every possible question at once.

More specific wording does not establish whether an answer is correct. It makes the intended question easier to identify, so the reader can compare the response with what they asked for.

The [handoff](https://metalearner-internship-logs.vercel.app/) records the implemented tour and guide. The useful division is between an introduction you pass through once and instructions you return to when a particular task needs them.
