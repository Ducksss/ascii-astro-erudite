SafeStep was a hackathon prototype for supporting older adults and their caregivers. It appears as AgeWell in the repository, and the public submission credits Anton Ang, Ivan Chua, Faith Lee and me. I worked on the backend, which was an early Node.js project for me.

We wanted to reduce repeated checking around meals, medication and safety. The proposed system would observe routine activities, keep a checklist and give caregivers useful context. Building it exposed a problem with that proposal: seeing something happen and knowing that a care task is complete are different things.

## Two views of the same day

The senior-facing screen emphasised the current time, a daily checklist and large actions for navigation and help. The caregiver screen held a denser timeline, task progress, alerts and a location map.

That split was worth keeping. Someone trying to find their way home needs a clear next action, while a caregiver reviewing the day needs context and timestamps. Putting all of that on one screen would make both tasks harder.

The implementation was less complete than the screenshots might suggest. Both dashboards used their own sample state. The camera page could open a live webcam, but its activity and fall buttons simulated detections with fixed confidence values. Those screens demonstrated interactions, without demonstrating the whole proposed care flow.

## The backend's consequential shortcut

The [Express server](https://github.com/Ducksss/hack4good/blob/a8948fc101dbc1a9af251ec18316fd39aec791a0/backend/server.js) provided routes for activities, tasks, location, fall alerts and a weekly summary. A separate Python service could post detected activities to it.

Receiving an activity appended a timestamped record and completed a matching task. For an eating event, the server chose breakfast, lunch or dinner from the hour of day. For medication, it selected the first unfinished medication task.

```text wrap=false
activity label -> timestamped record -> matching task marked complete
```

It was a small amount of code, which made the intended flow easy to inspect. It also gave the label too much authority. The event's confidence was stored, but did not decide whether the task should be completed. The task selection did not establish which meal was eaten or which medication was taken.

The server stored its data in memory, so restarting it lost the activity history. The weekly summary returned fixed demonstration values. These were prototype choices, rather than a basis for reporting someone's health or medication adherence.

## What an object label can establish

The [Python experiment](https://github.com/Ducksss/hack4good/blob/a8948fc101dbc1a9af251ec18316fd39aec791a0/ai-service/app.py) used YOLOv8 to detect objects, then mapped labels into activities. Cutlery, food and cups could suggest eating. A bottle could suggest medication or hydration.

That mapping contains ambiguity before any model error enters the picture. A cup on a table does not establish that someone drank from it. A bottle does not establish that the right person took the right dose. In this implementation, the category order could classify a cup as eating or a bottle as medication before reaching hydration.

A high confidence in the object label would not resolve that ambiguity, because confidence that an object is a bottle is different from evidence that medication was taken.

The fall endpoint was also a simulation, and the alert route returned a success message without making an emergency call. It would be misleading to treat either as validated fall detection or a completed emergency response.

## Less checking still needs consent

Our aim was to make support feel less intrusive, but a camera and location feed do not become private simply because their interface is quiet. They can reduce the need for phone calls while increasing how much of someone's day is observable.

The prototype did not resolve who could see that information, when monitoring should stop or how the person receiving care could control it. Those questions belong in the interaction itself, including a visible monitoring state, an accessible way to pause it and clear control over sharing.

I would also change the status model before adding more detection features. An observation could be shown as **possible activity**, then confirmed or corrected by a person, instead of immediately becoming **task complete**. A missed observation should remain unknown rather than automatically mean a missed meal.

That would make the dashboard less reassuring at a glance, but more useful to someone deciding whether to check in. The valuable record is one that shows what was observed, what was confirmed and what remains uncertain.
